Constraint costs

What NESO spends each day keeping flows within the network’s limits, mostly paying Scottish wind to stop and plants further south to start. Published about a week in arrears.

How this works

Generators tell the market what they plan to produce, then offer prices for producing more (offers) and for producing less (bids). When NESO needs a change, because demand is different from the plans or a line would overload, it accepts the cheapest suitable bids and offers.

Wind farms usually bid a negative price, because they lose subsidy payments when they stop, so NESO pays them to turn down. The cables from Scotland to England can’t carry everything Scottish wind farms produce on a windy day, so NESO pays them to stop and pays gas stations nearer demand to start. That’s the biggest share of constraint costs.

“Right now” comes from the instructions published in the last few minutes, priced at each unit’s first bid or offer for this half hour, so the cost per hour is an estimate. Finished half hours use Elexon’s indicative cashflows. NESO also spends money outside this market, through trades and contracts, which isn’t included.
