Constraints

Most of Britain’s wind is in Scotland and the North Sea, and most of its demand is in England’s cities. When the lines in between can’t carry it all, NESO pays wind farms to turn down and power stations further south to turn up. This page shows where, how often, and what it costs.

        How this works

        The transmission network is planned around boundaries: lines across the map where a set of circuits carries power from one part of the country to another. B4 separates the north of Scotland from the Central Belt; B6 runs along the border with England. Each has a limit, set by how much the circuits can carry safely if one of them fails.

        The flows here are NESO’s forecasts, made the day before, of what would flow if every power station ran as the market scheduled it. Since April 2024 they leave out the actions NESO takes to manage constraints, so a flow above 100% means NESO expects to step in: paying wind farms on one side to turn down (their bids) and generators on the other side to turn up (their offers). The wind farms are paid for the subsidy and income they give up, which is why constraints cost so much on windy days.

        NESO publishes each boundary group by a code. Where the meaning is clear, this page gives it a name; the others keep NESO’s code. Only B4 and B6 are drawn in colour on the map, as they’re the only boundaries whose groups match them certainly; the rest are drawn faintly for reference.

        Wind turned down comes from NESO’s record of accepted bids from wind farms, which counts every reason for turning them down, though nearly all are constraints. Costs are NESO’s daily constraint costs, published a few days after the day.

        Data: NESO’s Day Ahead Constraint Flows and Limits, Thermal Constraint Costs, Constraint Breakdown and Wind BOA Volumes, and the ETYS transmission boundaries, under the NESO Open Data Licence.
