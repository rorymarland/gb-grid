How this works

The dashboard asks Open-Meteo for the weather every hour at a few dozen places and turns each measure into one figure for Britain. Wind is the wind speed 100 metres up, about the height of a turbine’s hub, at a dozen points among the wind farms, each counted by the capacity of the farms nearest it. Temperature is the air temperature in each English region, Scotland and Wales, counted by how many people live there (the ONS’s mid-2022 estimates). Sunshine is the sunlight reaching the ground, in watts per square metre averaged over the hour, in each of NESO’s 14 regions, counted by how much solar power each produced over the last week.

The past two days are Open-Meteo’s most recent forecasts for those hours, which follow the measurements closely; the rest is its forecast. The first time it runs, the dashboard asks for the last 92 days as well, so the charts below have something to show straight away.

Against the wind, the share of their capacity that transmission-connected wind farms made each hour, plus what NESO paid them to turn down, so constraints don’t hide the wind. Against the temperature, each day’s average national demand, with weekends apart because businesses use less; the figure for each degree is a straight line through working days below 15.5°C, where heating takes over. Against the sunshine, solar output each hour, as NESO records it in its historic generation mix.
